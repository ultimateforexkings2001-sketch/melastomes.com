Axinaea: The Andean Genus Pollinated by Birds

M
Melastomes.com Editorial
•July 14, 2026•4 min read

Most melastomes rely on bees to shake pollen out of their flowers. Axinaea, a genus of shrubs and small trees from the cloud forests of the Andes, took a different route and recruited birds.

Quick facts. Family: Melastomataceae. Range: mainly the northern and central Andes, with a few species in Central America. Habitat: humid montane forest, typically at middle to high elevations. Growth form: shrubs and small trees. Pollinators: small birds, especially tanagers and similar species.

Where Axinaea grows

Axinaea is a Neotropical genus of several dozen species. Its centre of diversity runs through Colombia, Ecuador and Peru, where mist-drenched slopes between roughly 1,500 and 3,500 metres provide the cool, constantly humid conditions it favours. Like many Andean melastomes, it often grows at forest edges, along landslides and in other gaps where light reaches the shrub layer.

The squeeze-box stamens

The feature that made Axinaea famous among pollination biologists is its stamens. In most melastomes the anthers open through small apical pores, and a visiting bee vibrates the flower to shake pollen out (see buzz pollination). In Axinaea, the connective, the tissue joining the anther sacs, is extended into a swollen, fleshy appendage that is rich in starch and sugars.

Researchers who have watched these flowers in the field describe a mechanism that works rather like a bellows. When a bird pecks at or grasps the inflated appendage, the squeeze forces pollen out through the anther pores and onto the bird's head or bill. The bird gets a snack, and the plant gets its pollen moved to the next flower. Because the reward is part of the stamen itself rather than nectar, this is sometimes described as a food-body pollination system.

Why this matters scientifically

Bird pollination has evolved repeatedly in the Neotropics, and melastomes offer some of the clearest examples of how a pollination mechanism can be rebuilt from existing parts. Axinaea did not invent a new organ. It modified a structure already present in the family, the stamen connective, and repurposed it. This is a textbook case of how complex adaptations are usually assembled from available material rather than from scratch.

Appearance

Axinaea flowers are typically showy, with bright petals that are often yellow to orange or reddish, depending on the species. The leaves show the three to five strong longitudinal veins characteristic of the family, and are usually opposite, leathery and sometimes covered with fine hairs or scales (see how to identify a melastome).

Cultivation

Axinaea is rarely seen in cultivation. Species from the high Andes need cool nights, constant moisture and high humidity, conditions that are difficult to reproduce in a typical home or temperate garden. Where it is grown, it is generally by specialists and botanic gardens with cloud-forest conditions.

Conservation

Andean cloud forests have been heavily cleared for agriculture and pasture, and narrow-ranged species are particularly exposed. Several Axinaea species have small distributions, which makes habitat protection the most important conservation measure. See conservation of melastomes for the wider picture.

Key takeaways

  • Axinaea is an Andean genus of shrubs and small trees.
  • Its stamens carry inflated, food-rich appendages.
  • Birds squeeze these appendages, which releases pollen onto the bird.
  • It is a good example of the family's pollination diversity, which ranges from buzzing bees to birds and even bats and rodents in some Andean lineages.
